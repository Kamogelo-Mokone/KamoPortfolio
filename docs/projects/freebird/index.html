WEBSITE

Freebird

A modern platform concept designed to connect freelancers with opportunities through a streamlined search experience and visually engaging interface.

Product Area
Two-sided freelance marketplace
Focus
Landing page & job discovery
Role
UI/UX designer
Scope
Homepage, search, categories & trust
Status
Concept — desktop design

DESIGN DECISIONS

Deeper Context

  1. A search-first entry point speaks to both sides of the marketplace. Visitors can identify as a freelancer looking for work or choose the type of work they need, then move straight into search.
  2. A playful visual identity uses a bright illustrated character, colourful accents and a soft teal-to-lime gradient to make the experience feel energetic and approachable.
  3. Category exploration is treated as visual browsing. Image-led category tiles make different kinds of work easier to scan than a plain list.
  4. Success stories and visible ratings add social proof near the discovery experience, helping new visitors understand the scale and credibility of the community.
  5. The homepage brings search, categories, community stories and newsletter signup into one guided journey, while keeping the primary job-search action easy to find.

Why This Matters

For the business. A marketplace lives or dies on liquidity, meaning enough freelancers and enough clients showing up and finding each other. The homepage is the main place that gets decided. By giving each audience its own section and offering search at both the top and bottom of the page, the design reduces the chance that a visitor leaves without taking a step toward signing up or searching.

For freelancers. Many freelancers feel that platforms treat them as interchangeable. Framing the product around work they “actually love,” showing real success stories, and using warm, human imagery speaks to motivation and fit, not just job listings.

For clients. Companies, individuals and local businesses all have different anxieties: speed and reliability, budget and ease, and trust and proximity, respectively. Addressing these separately shows that the platform understands who is using it.

For the market. The design carries a South African identity through its flags and its local business angle. A platform that treats local businesses as a first-class audience can build a niche that global competitors often overlook.

For me as a designer. This project shows end-to-end thinking on a single, content-heavy page: information hierarchy, a consistent colour system for actions, audience segmentation, and layouts that balance imagery with copy. It demonstrates that I can take a business idea and turn it into a clear, scannable, conversion-minded interface.

Reflection

What worked well.

Clear hierarchy and rhythm.

The page alternates between tinted bands (mint blue for social proof and category browsing) and white sections, which gives it a natural reading rhythm and clear section breaks.

Consistent action colour.

Lime is reserved for calls to action and interactive controls, so users learn quickly what to click.

Segmented storytelling.

The two-audience zig-zag layout keeps a long page engaging and avoids a one-size-fits-all pitch.

Search in the right places.

Putting search in the hero and again before the category grid supports both decisive and exploratory visitors.

What I would improve or revisit.

Content consistency.

The design currently shows slightly conflicting figures: “Over 4200 freelancers” in the hero and “Over 5,000 Active Jobs” in the success stories and category sections. These need clean, verified numbers (and a typo fix for “4000”) before launch.

Placeholder content.

The testimonial text is lorem ipsum, the company logo slots are empty grey blocks, and the filter button reads “Button”. Real copy and labels are needed to judge how the layout holds up with actual content lengths.

Repeated hero copy.

The hero headline is repeated almost word for word in the second section. That space could carry a different message, such as how it works or a key differentiator.

Filter labelling.

Two dropdowns are both labelled “Category”, which could confuse users. They should be differentiated (for example, Category, Experience level, and Work type).

Text legibility.

The body copy in the audience sections is quite small and light grey against white, which may fall short of accessibility contrast guidelines. Test against WCAG standards and increase size and contrast.

Hero subtext spacing.

The hero subtext lines sit very tightly together and could use more line height to match the breathing room elsewhere.

Responsive and interaction states.

This is a desktop layout only. Next steps should be mobile and tablet versions, hover and focus states for the tiles and dropdowns, and logged-in experiences for freelancers and clients.

What I learned.

A marketplace homepage has to do many jobs at once: explain the idea, build trust, and move different users toward different actions. The most useful decision was to design around the audiences first (freelancer, company, individual, local business) and let the layout follow from that. If I continued this project, I would validate the messaging with real freelancers and clients and measure which entry point (hero search, category tiles, or the lower search) actually drives the most sign-ups.
